Desk Stretches for Office Workers: A 5-Minute Routine

A short, no-equipment sequence for breaking up screen time and moving your neck, shoulders, back, hips, and wrists during a busy workday.

Published 2026-06-24 · Updated 2026-10-05 · By DeskWarriorFit
Move

Desk stretches for office workers do not need special equipment or a long workout window. This five-minute routine gives you a simple way to change position after focused computer work and gently move areas that may feel stiff after sitting. You can do most of it beside your desk; stand only if you have room and feel steady.

There is no single “perfect” posture to hold all day. Changing positions, taking brief movement breaks, and adjusting a workstation to fit you are more practical goals. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration’s computer-workstation guidance recommends varying tasks and taking short breaks from computer work.

A five-minute desk stretch sequence

Move slowly and keep each motion comfortable. Use a chair or desk for balance when standing, and skip any movement that does not suit your body.

  1. Shoulder rolls — 30 seconds. Sit or stand tall. Gently roll your shoulders up, back, and down six to eight times, then reverse direction. Keep your jaw relaxed and avoid forcing the range.
  2. Chest opener — 30 seconds. Stand with your feet stable. Bring your hands behind you or rest them on your hips, then draw your shoulders gently back as you lift your chest. Breathe normally; do not push your ribs forward or strain your lower back.
  3. Upper-back turns — 1 minute. Sit toward the front of a stable chair with feet on the floor. Cross your arms comfortably over your chest and turn your upper body a little to one side, return to center, then switch. Repeat slowly for about 30 seconds each way.
  4. Standing hip-front stretch — 1 minute. Hold the desk or chair for support. Step one foot back, soften both knees, and shift your weight forward slightly until you feel a mild stretch at the front of the back hip. Hold about 30 seconds, then change sides. Keep the movement small and steady.
  5. Wrist and hand reset — 1 minute. Let your arms rest by your sides. Open and close your hands several times, then gently turn your palms up and down. If comfortable, extend one arm with the palm facing forward and use the other hand to ease the fingers back slightly; switch sides after 15 seconds. Never pull hard on the fingers or wrist.
  6. Easy walk or posture change — 1 minute. Walk to refill water or take a few steps away from the screen. If you cannot leave your desk, stand briefly or shift to a comfortable new position.

A low-friction reset

Pair the routine with a task you already do—after a meeting, while a file loads, or before lunch. A short, repeatable pause is easier to fit into a real workday than waiting for a perfect workout break.

Make movement breaks work with your day

Use the sequence when your body feels ready for a change, rather than treating the clock as a strict prescription. You could do the full five minutes once, or spread a shoulder roll, a short walk, and the hip stretch across a few natural pauses. If you are in a long meeting, standing briefly or changing how you sit still counts as a position change.

A simple cue can make the habit easier to remember: take a few steps every time you finish a call, or stretch after completing a task. Keep the movements gentle and comfortable. These stretches are a general movement break, not a diagnosis or treatment for an injury, and they do not replace regular physical activity outside work.

Make the routine fit the space you have. In a shared office, choose small movements that do not need much room; at home, you may have space to step away from your desk for the hip stretch or a short walk. If standing is uncomfortable or unsafe for you, leave out the standing movements and choose a comfortable seated position change instead.

You do not need to complete every move each time. Pick one or two that feel useful, then return to work. The goal is a manageable pause from staying still—not a test of flexibility or a requirement to stretch through discomfort.

Check your setup and listen to your body

Stretching can feel good, but repeatedly reaching for the same tight area may not address what is making work uncomfortable. Keep your keyboard and mouse close enough that your shoulders can relax, place the screen where you can view it without leaning forward, and adjust your chair so you feel supported. Revisit one adjustment at a time and notice whether it makes the work position easier.

A stretch should feel mild, not sharp or painful. Stop if you notice pain, tingling, numbness, dizziness, or symptoms that get worse. If discomfort persists, keeps returning, or interferes with daily activities, ask a qualified healthcare professional for personalized advice before continuing a routine.

Your workday checklist

Change position during long computer tasks, keep each stretch comfortable, use a steady support for standing moves, and repeat the routine at a natural transition. Skip anything that causes symptoms and get individualized guidance for persistent discomfort.
