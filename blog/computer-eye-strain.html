How to Reduce Computer Eye Strain: A Desk Worker’s Guide

Simple screen, lighting, and break adjustments to make long computer days more comfortable—without adding another complicated routine.

Published 2026-10-04
Recover

By mid-afternoon, the screen can start to feel too bright, words may seem harder to focus on, and your eyes may feel dry or tired. These are common complaints after long stretches of close-up screen work. They do not mean you have done permanent damage, but they are a useful sign to change how you are working.

Computer eye strain—often called digital eye strain—can have several contributors: long periods of near focus, reduced blinking, glare, small text, and an uncomfortable viewing setup. There is no single setting that fixes everything. A few small changes, repeated throughout the workday, are a more practical place to start.

Use the 20-20-20 rule as a reminder to pause

Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for at least 20 seconds. This familiar guideline gives your eyes a brief change from focusing at screen distance. It is not a medical treatment or a substitute for an eye exam, but it is an easy cue to interrupt uninterrupted close work.

Make the pause fit your day. Look out a window, focus on something across the room, or stand to refill your water. If you tend to lose track of time, attach the break to a recurring meeting, a calendar reminder, or a natural stopping point in your task. A reminder you will actually notice is better than a perfect schedule you ignore.

A low-friction reset

At the next task transition, look away from your screen, blink normally, and let your shoulders drop. Twenty seconds is a useful start; longer movement breaks can help you reset the rest of your body too.

Set up your screen for comfortable viewing

You should be able to read without leaning toward the display or squinting. Put the screen directly in front of you, roughly an arm’s length away, then adjust the text size and viewing angle until your head and neck can stay relaxed. Many people find it comfortable when the screen sits slightly below eye level, so the eyes look gently downward.

  • Increase text size before leaning in. Use display scaling or browser zoom if small text makes you crane your neck.
  • Reduce reflections. Turn the screen away from direct windows or bright lamps, and adjust blinds or lighting where possible.
  • Match brightness to the room. If the display feels like a light source in a dim room—or looks washed out in bright light—adjust screen brightness and nearby lighting.
  • Keep the screen clean. Smudges and dust can make text less crisp and encourage squinting.

If you work mainly on a laptop, a stand plus an external keyboard and mouse can make it easier to raise the screen without lifting your shoulders. For more workspace adjustments, see our home office ergonomics guide.

Remember to blink and let your eyes get a real break

People often blink less while concentrating on a screen. That can leave the eyes feeling dry or irritated. Make a point of blinking fully during screen breaks, and avoid aiming a fan or air-conditioning vent directly at your face. If dryness keeps returning, ask an eye-care professional whether lubricating eye drops are appropriate for you.

The 20-20-20 pause is brief, so do not treat it as your only break. After a long stretch of computer work, step away for a few minutes if your schedule allows. A short walk or a gentle desk stretch can also ease the shoulder and neck tension that tends to build while focusing.

Do blue-light glasses solve computer eye strain?

Blue-light-blocking glasses are often marketed as a screen-fatigue fix, but they are not a reliable substitute for breaks, comfortable screen positioning, or an up-to-date vision prescription. If you are considering a new pair because your eyes feel uncomfortable, talk with an eye-care professional about what may be contributing to your symptoms instead of assuming blue light is the cause.

When to get professional advice

Take persistent discomfort seriously. If symptoms continue despite adjusting your setup and taking breaks, or you have blurry vision, headaches, or eye pain, contact an optometrist or ophthalmologist. An uncorrected vision issue or another condition may be contributing, and a clinician can recommend the right next step. Seek prompt care for sudden changes in vision or severe pain.

Your practical checklist

Try a 20-20-20 reminder, move the display to a comfortable distance, enlarge hard-to-read text, reduce glare, and take a real break between long work blocks. Start with one change today and keep the ones that make your workday feel better.
