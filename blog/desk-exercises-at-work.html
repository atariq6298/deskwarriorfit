Desk Exercises You Can Do at Your Desk

A few simple seated and standing moves can break up long screen sessions and help you feel less stuck—without changing clothes or leaving the office.

Published 2026-10-06 · By DeskWarriorFit
Movement breaks

Desk exercises you can do at your desk are a practical way to add movement to a day packed with calls, messages, and deadlines. You do not need to turn your workspace into a gym or perform a full workout between tasks. A brief, comfortable change of position can simply interrupt a long spell of sitting.

Think of these moves as options, not a checklist you have to complete perfectly. Choose one or two that suit your space and body, breathe normally, and keep the range easy. If a movement causes pain, tingling, numbness, or dizziness, stop and leave it out.

Try these easy seated desk exercises

Start with a stable chair and enough room to move your arms. Keep your feet supported and make every motion smooth; there is no need to force a stretch or compete for extra range.

  1. Shoulder circles — 20 seconds. Sit or stand comfortably. Draw both shoulders up, gently back, and down several times, then reverse direction. Let your arms hang loose instead of bracing them.
  2. Seated upper-body turns — 30 seconds. Sit with both feet on the floor and your hands resting on your thighs. Turn your chest a little to one side while keeping your hips facing forward, return to center, then switch. Keep your gaze easy and stop before the turn feels strained.
  3. Ankle pumps — 30 seconds. With your feet on the floor, lift your toes while keeping your heels down, then lift your heels while your toes stay down. Repeat at a comfortable pace. This is an easy way to move your lower legs without standing up.
  4. Hand and wrist reset — 30 seconds. Let your hands rest, open and close your fingers a few times, and slowly turn your palms up and down. Keep wrists relaxed rather than stretching them hard.
  5. Gentle seated march — 30 seconds. Sit tall enough to feel steady and alternately lift each foot a small distance from the floor. If this does not feel comfortable, skip it and choose another movement.

That sequence takes about two minutes, but you do not have to do every exercise at once. For more ways to move while seated, see Healthline’s deskercise guide, which inspired this article’s focus on accessible office movement.

A no-fuss movement break

Pick one move and link it to a natural pause—after a meeting, while a page loads, or before you refill your water. A small break you remember is more useful than a complicated routine you keep postponing.

Add a standing option when it suits you

If you feel steady and have room, stand beside your chair for a brief change of position. Hold the desk for support if needed, keep your movements controlled, and avoid anything that feels awkward in your clothes or workspace.

  • Easy calf raises. Hold the back of a stable chair or desk, rise gently onto your toes, then lower your heels. Try a few repetitions and keep your weight balanced.
  • Supported leg bends. With a hand on your desk, bend one knee slightly and straighten again without locking it. Switch sides and use a small range that feels natural.
  • Short walk. Walk a few steps to a nearby window, printer, or water bottle. Walking is a simple alternative if stretching in the office feels distracting.

Standing is optional. If you cannot safely stand, prefer seated movement or simply shift to a more comfortable supported position. Shared offices and small work areas are different, so adapt the routine to the space and your access needs.

Make desk movement a habit that fits

There is no magic timer interval that works for everyone. Instead of aiming for a rigid schedule, look for repeatable moments in your workday: finishing a call, sending a report, getting up for lunch, or switching projects. A calendar reminder can help if you often work through those transitions without noticing.

Keep the bar low. You might do one shoulder circle after a video meeting, take a short walk after a long focus block, and use ankle pumps while reading. These small changes are not a substitute for regular physical activity, but they can help break up stretches spent in one position.

It can also help to prepare the workspace so movement is easy: move bags out of the way, keep a clear path for a short walk, and place your water where you will notice it. If you share an office, choose discreet moves that feel comfortable around other people. The best routine is one that works in your real environment.

Your quick deskercise checklist

Choose one seated move, add a standing option only if it feels safe, and connect your break to something you already do. Breathe normally, stay in a comfortable range, and stop if symptoms start or get worse.

These are general movement ideas, not a treatment plan. If discomfort persists, keeps returning, or interferes with your everyday activities, ask a qualified healthcare professional for guidance tailored to you.
